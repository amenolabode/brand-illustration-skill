# Research and translation

## Research sequence

### Meaning and provenance

Search:

- `[source] history meaning symbolism`
- `[source] cultural context archive museum`
- `[source] makers terminology techniques`
- `[source] sacred restricted commercial use`

Record origin, geography, function, makers, dates and whether the source carries protected, sacred or community-specific meaning.

### Construction and behavior

Search:

- `how [source] is made process diagram`
- `[source] geometry repeat unit structure`
- `[source] material cross section close up`
- `[source] motion growth transformation stages`
- `[field] technical drawing manual notation`
- `[field] taxonomy atlas specimen sheet`

Collect process diagrams, close-ups, tools, intermediate states and failed or irregular examples. They reveal more generative rules than polished finished specimens.

### Translation and feasibility

Search:

- `[process] generative design algorithm`
- `[pattern] minimum line weight print screen`
- `[source] animation principles movement`
- `[source] practitioners collaboration licensing`

Test whether the source can be simplified without losing its identity or meaning.

## Candidate source territories

Use these as prompts, not presets:

- Weaving and basketry: interdependence, continuity; over/under logic, tension, edge binding.
- Cartography: orientation and progress; contours, routes, scale, coordinates and legends.
- Botanical growth: care and compounding; branching, rings, veins, seasonal states and density gradients.
- Geology: time and foundations; strata, faults, compression, erosion, cores and cross-sections.
- Engineering drawings: clarity and transparency; grids, exploded views, section cuts and dimensions.
- Music notation: coordination and tempo; measures, rests, accents, repetition and progression.
- Ceramics and joinery: craft and durability; profiles, seams, joints, scoring and assembly.
- Networks and circuitry: connection and flow; nodes, traces, routing, gates and state changes.
- Astronomy: orientation and scale; orbits, phases, parallax, constellations and coordinate systems.
- Archival classification: trust and retrieval; stamps, indices, tabs, fields, registration and annotation.

Prefer a source with both a strong semantic reason and enough native operations to generate varied assets.

## Candidate scorecard

Score 1–5 and explain each score:

- Brand-semantic fit.
- Product relevance.
- Ownability within the category.
- Number of generative operations.
- Range across functional and emotional subjects.
- Small-size reduction.
- Motion potential.
- Production feasibility.
- Accessibility.
- Provenance and permission confidence.

Reject candidates with strong aesthetics but weak meaning or limited range.

## Translation worksheet

### Strategic input

- Tenet: what must always be true?
- Verb: what transformation occurs?
- Tension: which qualities must coexist?
- Audience effect: what becomes clearer, easier or more valuable?

### Source logic

- Function: why does the source exist?
- Process: how is it made or generated?
- Native operations: what does it repeatedly do?
- Associations: what does it already signify?

### Visual grammar

- Primitives.
- Relationships.
- Parameters.
- Invariants.
- Prohibited shortcuts.

### Application system

- Symbol layer: fastest-readable subject.
- Brand layer: ownable grammar.
- Spatial layer: composition, field and depth.
- Behavior layer: source-native motion.

## Cultural stewardship

For a culturally specific source:

1. Name the actual community or tradition; avoid umbrella labels.
2. Use primary or community-led sources.
3. Determine whether motifs convey identity, rank, ceremony, religion or restricted knowledge.
4. Involve practitioners when the form is central to the commercial identity.
5. Attribute, license and compensate as appropriate.
6. Document what was transformed and what was deliberately not used.

If provenance cannot be established, choose another source or use a generic process that does not claim cultural specificity.

## Reference analysis

Use this when the user shares an existing illustration system (images, GIFs, a site) and asks what drives it or how to make something like it.

1. **Look at every frame.** For animated files, extract several frames: motion often reveals the construction states (wireframe → volume → material) and what the motion explains.
2. **Separate the modes.** Most systems combine two or three rendering modes, such as a technical scene, flat pictograms and extruded objects. Describe each one separately: projection, fills, edges, pattern placement.
3. **Measure, don't guess.** Sample the palette, and note the pattern's line weight relative to the silhouette, its contrast against the face, its density and where it sits.
4. **Name the source and verify it.** Hypothesise the source world from the pattern's behaviour, then confirm it with the designer's or studio's own published rationale. State clearly what is confirmed and what is inferred.
5. **List the execution qualities** a new system must keep when the source changes (SKILL.md, Phase 6). This list becomes the checklist for the side-by-side review.
6. **Describe the layer-by-layer reconstruction** for each mode, so the analysis can be checked by rebuilding it.

`cowrywise-example.md` is a complete analysis of this kind.
